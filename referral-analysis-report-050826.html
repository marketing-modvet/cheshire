Headline finding: Internal referral activity (GP clinics sending cases to Hospital/BOVC/TVRC) is real, sizeable, and growing — it is one of the most active appointment categories in the entire system. However, the practice currently has no way to measure or track referrals sent OUT to other businesses (external leakage). If that is a genuine concern, it needs a new operational habit before it can be measured, not a data-mining exercise on the current system.

This is the biggest finding in the whole analysis: nearly two-thirds (65%) of real GP-to-specialist patient movements are happening with zero use of the referral system at all — no Referral appointment type, no mention of "referred" in either visit's notes. Only about 3 in 10 movements actually use the formal Referral appointment types that already exist in ezyVet. This means the practice's own referral reporting (and this analysis's Section 4 rate) is likely undercounting real referral activity for almost every doctor — the system just isn't being used consistently to log it, regardless of whether the clinical handoff itself is happening correctly.

Outbound (cases WE send elsewhere)

No structured tracking exists. Checked consult records, appointment types, communication templates, and free-text notes — none of them log "this case was referred out to another practice." This isn't a data quality issue to fix with better queries; it's a workflow that has never been captured in the system.

Recommendation: if outbound leakage is a genuine strategic concern, the fix is operational — e.g. a required consult tag, a dedicated appointment type, or even a simple shared log — not a deeper data-mining pass on the current system.

6. Missed-Referral Detection: A Methodology Test

We tested whether "silent missed referral" cases (a serious condition presented, no referral given, and a later visit suggesting the problem quietly resolved — possibly treated outside) could be surfaced automatically from clinical notes.

Honest result: this approach is not reliable enough to present as findings. Manual review of all 9 top candidates showed mostly false positives — e.g. a "cruciate tear, no referral" case that was actually already being actively managed with a physiotherapist, and a "seizure resolved" match where the actual note was "not sure if it was a seizure, but stable now" (still ambiguous). Free-text veterinary notes are too inconsistent for keyword-matching alone. The detection concept works mechanically, but every candidate needs a clinician's eyes before it means anything.

By specialty (raw candidate counts): neurology 230, oncology 226, surgery/ortho 131, ophthalmology 61, cardiology 30, dermatology 9. 7. Recommendations

  1. Internal referrals are healthy and measurable — no action needed beyond periodic monitoring of the trend already established here.
  2. Confirm the April 2026 referral-type change was intentional and, if so, consider retiring the generic "Referral" type in favor of the more specific sub-types going forward for cleaner future reporting.
  3. Check Mobile Clinic's referral workflow — it's using "Internal" almost exclusively rather than the formal Referral types used everywhere else.
  4. If external/outbound leakage matters to leadership, build the tracking first. A single new consult tag or appointment type would make this measurable within a quarter.
  5. Missed-referral detection needs a human pass, not more automation. If this is worth pursuing, the 687 raw candidates are ready for a clinician to triage — that's a clinical governance exercise, not a data project.
