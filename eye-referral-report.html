What this report is (and isn't)

This is a candidate-screening pass, not a clinical audit. Every animal below is flagged because of a data pattern — a drug that's typically used for chronic eye disease, or specific wording in an appointment/consult note — combined with no eye surgery ever billed for that animal in our system. That combination is a reasonable proxy for "this looks like it could have needed a specialist referral, and we have no record of surgery happening here" — but it does not confirm a referral was missed. Legitimate explanations include: the animal was referred out and we simply don't capture that outcome, the medication was managing the condition without surgery being indicated, the surgery happened at another clinic before joining us, or the case resolved with non-surgical management. Every row here needs a human (ideally a vet) to open the actual chart before drawing conclusions.

Known data gaps carried over from the general report: there is no structured field capturing referrals sent OUT of our clinics, and consult/appointment notes are short free-text (no full SOAP notes were available to search) — so text-based signals will always under-count real cases that were phrased differently, and this pass can only see what made it into a description field.

Methodology

Andrew asked for 5 specific identifiers. Here's exactly how each was built and what it found:

Signal 1 — Medications related to chronic eye issues

Classified all 701 eye-related products in the full 52,211-product catalog into medication (573) vs. surgery (128) vs. diagnostic/irrelevant buckets, then pulled every invoiceline ever billed against the medication products. Flagged an animal if it was ever dispensed a drug specific to chronic eye disease management — glaucoma maintenance drops (dorzolamide/timolol, latanoprost, brinzolamide/azopt), or immune-mediated dry-eye/KCS treatment (ciclosporin/cyclosporine, tacrolimus, Optimmune) — with no eye surgery invoiceline on file, ever (not just within the 14-month window — full billing history).

Result: 1,198 animals. This is intentionally broad — a chronic glaucoma drop regimen with no surgery is often clinically appropriate (medical management is a legitimate first line), so this signal is best read as a screening list, not a red-flag list on its own.

Signal 2 — "Post-op" mention without any surgery on invoice history

Searched appointment/consult descriptions for "post-op"/"post op"/"postoperative" occurring alongside eye-related context words (eye, ocular, cornea, conjunctiva, glaucoma, cataract, retina, etc.), then excluded any animal with an eye surgery invoiceline anywhere in their history.

Result: 4 animals. Small but high-precision — a genuine "post-op eye check" note with nothing billed here strongly suggests the surgery happened elsewhere (referred out, or done before joining us) and is worth a quick chart check to confirm the outcome was actually followed up.

Signal 3 — Glaucoma / cataract mention

Searched all appointment/consult descriptions for the words "glaucoma" or "cataract" anywhere. 64 animals had at least one mention; of those, 60 have no eye surgery ever billed. This is the most directly actionable signal — several of these are explicit notes like "Estimate for Cataract Surgery" or "ref by Dr. Horacio" with no follow-through visible in billing.

Signal 4 — Typical post-op eye medication as identifier

Looked for the classic post-surgical eye regimen pattern — an antibiotic eye drop/ointment (chloramphenicol, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin family, etc.) and an anti-inflammatory eye drop (prednisolone, dexamethasone, NSAID family) dispensed to the same animal within a 5-day window — with no eye surgery invoiceline on file. This combo is a strong proxy for "a procedure happened and we're managing the aftermath" even without an explicit surgery line item (e.g. it was billed under a generic consult fee, or performed elsewhere and we're just doing the med refill).

Result: 2,648 animals. Also broad on its own (this drug combo is also used for plain eye infections/uveitis without surgery), which is why it's weighted much more heavily in this report when it co-occurs with Signal 2, 3, or 5.

Signal 5 — Emergency/urgent surgery mention without surgery performed and invoiced

Searched for "emergency"/"urgent"/"ASAP" co-occurring with acute eye-crisis language (eyeball out, cannot open eye, eye bleeding, corneal rupture/perforation, proptosis, enucleation, glaucoma, cataract, "eye sx"), excluding animals with a billed eye surgery.

Result: 7 animals. Very small, very high-precision — these read like genuine acute emergencies (e.g. a street-cat attack with the eye affected, a proptosis-sounding presentation) where the front-desk/triage note describes urgency but there's no matching surgical invoiceline. Worth a direct chart pull on each one.

Priority weighting used below: the 22 animals with an explicit text mention (Signal 2, 3, or 5) plus a medication-pattern signal (1 or 4) are listed first as the highest-confidence review list. The remaining 389 animals were flagged by medication pattern only (Signals 1/4 co-occurring, no explicit text) — useful as a broader screening pool but lower precision individually.
